# Organizing Questions

My research first began with a few basic questions like how do AI systems work, how do experts test them, and what data supports their claims? 
To answer these questions, I put together an approach to examine AI from three perspectives of the sources like  real world testing, how it impacts human learning, and the technical reasons why it makes mistakes.

# sources and search procedures

The Economist was the most helpful source, with enough information for four of my notes. 
It stood out because the writers tested the AI rather than just stating claims. For example, they showed how the model failed to find a $20 whiskey statistic from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. 
Because the article gave specific, proven examples of AI failures, I had actual data to write about.

My focus moved from whether the technology works to whether it should be used. Ethan Mollick's article was best for this. 
Mollick research was on the human side, while newspapers focused on facts and flaws. He pointed out why struggle with a topic is necessary for learning, filling a gap in my research on how AI affects education and critical thinking.

Finally, The New York Times helped me understand the mechanics of AI errors. 
Instead of relying on claims from tech executives rather than scientific research, it was the only source that described why AI makes stuff up. 
This updated my notes from simply listing AI flaws to showing the weaknesses in how these models predict words and try to reason step by step.

# Questions

After finishing these notes, my biggest question is about the actual application of Ai. 
The sources do a good job of describing when to use AI and when not to. My problem is they do not explain how to use it properly. 
Figuring out the best ways to push the AI and verify its work is still an open question for me.
